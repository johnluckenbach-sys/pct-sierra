## The Parking Problem

The Kennedy Meadows South trailhead has a finite number of parking spots and an infinite number of people who want to use them on a holiday Friday. I rolled in around 9 AM and the lot was already gone — cars nosed into every legal and semi-legal slot, a few brave souls parked along the shoulder in spots that seemed like future tow jobs.

I ended up starting slightly south of the main TH, which added maybe a half-mile and required some creative route-finding to link back to the PCT. Fine. Good character building.

## The Heat

May 27th in the southern Sierra at 6,000 feet sounds mild. It was not mild. By 10 AM the trail was baking in direct sun, the breeze that had felt refreshing at the car had completely stopped, and I was doing that thing where you check how much water you have every fifteen minutes.

The southern end of this section doesn't offer much shade. You're exposed, the granite reflects heat, and the trail is dusty in a way that coats your throat. I drank more in those first eight miles than I usually do in a full day in the mountains.

## The Big Bridge

The South Fork Kern River bridge is one of those spots that becomes a social gathering point on busy weekends. When I got there, it was full — thru-hikers airing out their feet, weekenders refilling water, a dog that had clearly been on trail for weeks judging by its thousand-yard stare.

I sat in whatever shade I could find and ate lunch. The thru-hikers moved with the relaxed efficiency of people who have reduced their lives to forward motion. The weekenders, myself included, looked slightly more chaotic. Everyone was friendly. The river was cold and I put my feet in it for ten minutes before convincing myself to keep moving.

## Short of the Goal

The plan was to push to the Olancha Pass trail junction. The plan did not survive contact with the heat and the miles.

By mid-afternoon I'd covered enough ground to know I wasn't covering that much more. I found a campsite with some flat ground and what felt like slightly cooler air, set up my tent, and accepted that the junction would have to wait. It was one of those moments where the gap between what you planned and what your body is willing to do becomes undeniable.

This is the thing nobody tells you about section hiking on weekenders: you get there, and you're tired in a way that doesn't happen on weekday gym visits. The kind of tired that comes from heat and mileage and sustained effort over hours. It's clarifying.

I cooked dinner and was asleep before 9 PM.

## The Bathroom Situation

I'm going to talk about wilderness pooping because it's real and nobody prepares you properly.

My system going into this trip was underdeveloped. The southern Sierra soil near KMS is not always cooperating — you need six inches for a proper cat hole and some of this terrain is all root and rock. Add in the Memorial Day crowd density, the proximity to water, the Leave No Trace regulations I hadn't fully internalized, and I had a very educational experience.

I will say only: I came out of this trip with a WAG bag kit in my standard loadout, a better understanding of where on the landscape a cat hole is actually viable, and genuine respect for the people who write detailed LNT guidance.

Evolution through discomfort. That's the trail working as intended.

<Callout type="tip" title="Wilderness bathroom — the southern Sierra">
  Near KMS and in heavily-used areas, WAG bags are the move. Bring one even if you don't think you'll need it. The Restop 2 bags pack small and the consequences of not having one are worse than carrying it. Cat holes work further from the trail in areas with actual topsoil — 200 ft from water, 6" deep, pack out the TP.
</Callout>

## Day 2: Back

The return was easier. Cooler morning air, no crowd at the bridge yet, and the psychological lift of walking toward the car. I made decent time and was back to the trailhead by noon.

Drove down to the general store, got a cold drink, and sat in the parking lot for a while decompressing. The kind of sit where you're not looking at your phone or thinking about work, just being present in the specific physical relief of being done.

I'd planned to do more miles. I didn't. The trip was still worth exactly what it cost — a day of PTO, a tank of gas, and some genuine discomfort that reorganized a few of my backcountry assumptions.

More miles next time.
