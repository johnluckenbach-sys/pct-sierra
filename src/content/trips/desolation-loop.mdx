Covid and wildfires. The malaise of six months in lockdown. New and old jobs (all tech, and all high stress). I got Nick Frangos and Joe McGill to join me for an extended Labor Day loop throughout Desolation Wilderness. I had started my first "big boy job" in January, and was itching to do something outside after nine months on Annual Operating Plans, Replanning, re-replanning, budget versus actual, variance analysis, and spinning. At the time it felt stressful and unsustainable.

## Day 1

We had to have some finesse with the permits — Deso's permit system is zone-based, you can start at any trailhead, as long as you camp in your assigned zone on your first night. The obvious goal was Lake Aloha, but we got the Dick's Peak zone, which is directly adjacent. Nick and Joe were game to "skirt the line," so we aimed for Aloha. We started at Glen Alpine Trailhead — the drive from the Bay is easy, heading to Sac via I-80, then Highway 50 to South Lake. The final stretch is a frustrating stop-and-go on a one-way road directly adjacent to Fallen Leaf Lake.

Starting felt easy — we worked our way uphill, stopped for lunch (fresh Italian subs courtesy of Jen Frangos) at the PCT junction. Soon we were at Susie and Heather Lakes, and not long after, Aloha. I was anxious the whole way up that we'd find it overrun with hikers, and yet there we were, completely alone. A lovely grove sits at the very north end of the lake, suitable for probably ten or more tents.

Aloha is a man-made lake, filling a granite basin at the base of Mt. Price and Pyramid Peak. It's dotted with little islands, making it even more picturesque. We had a perfectly clear forecast for the entire trip, so Joe and I opted to just cowboy camp on a blue tarp, while Nick opted for a small one-man tent. I had a Mountain Hardwear "Mtn Speed 32," a narrow mummy bag with a tiny number-3 zipper that would constantly get stuck. We swam in the afternoon sun, jumping from granite islands into deep pools. As the sun set, we were greeted by a lovely alpenglow on Mt. Price and clear skies, stars abound.

## Day 2

I awoke a bit lethargic. It was a cold morning. Nick's little down quilt was not sufficient. We packed up and headed north to Mosquito Pass — a lovely little pass, easy to climb, strewn with boulders at the top, with epic views of Rockbound Valley and Clyde Lake. We descended briefly, then veered west up to Rockbound Pass, which is barren and gorgeous — like a mini High Sierra.

North again past Lake Lois and to Lake Schmidell. This lake is a special one: big, deep, with granite beaches abounding and easy, well-covered camping. I wanted to camp here, but Nick and Joe wanted to push onward. Back down into Rockbound Valley via an unmaintained trail, then up into Middle Velma Lake. I was feeling oddly gassed out — perhaps from months of desk work with little gym time.

Middle Velma is nice, but I liked Schmidell more. Velma is a confluence of trails — the Pacific Crest Trail, Tahoe Rim Trail, and easy weekender access from Eagle Falls — so it's busy. It's also at lower elevation with less granite, making the water murkier and the shores muddier. Still, it's a lovely place, with cute little tent pads sprinkled around the western shore. We played cards and cooked freeze-dried meals, and we were happy.

## Day 3

The smoke and haze were gradually increasing throughout the trip — on Day 3 we woke up to ash dusting our gear. Luckily we were on the back half, and the sunrise was especially colorful. We worked our way south on the PCT toward Dick's Pass. Passing through Fontanillis Lake, we saw families enjoying a lovely, clear morning, kids running about and parents cooking breakfast. It was idyllic and came as a complete surprise — a reminder that life does go on, and even in the middle of a pandemic these families found the time, energy, and resources to enjoy a pristine alpine lake with their children.

The slog up to the pass was hard, partially due to the smoky air choking our lungs. Eating lunch at the top, we watched firefighting helicopters ferry to and fro. Down to Gilmore by early afternoon. We nabbed a camp, then moved to accommodate a whole battalion of Boy Scouts training for Philmont. Nick talked to them about it — for some reason I didn't feel like it, like the heartache of mistakes made was still too fresh, and my words to these young men might taint an otherwise life-changing experience.

We side-hiked up to Mount Tallac, featuring expansive views of the South Tahoe region — a true and total slog of a climb. Joe tried to hit on a girl at the top, to no avail. Back down at Gilmore, we took one more swim, this one easily the coldest of the entire trip — more of a polar plunge than a summer dip. Two women set up directly adjacent to us; in truth, we were all probably camped too close to the shore, but there were no obvious alternatives.

The final night is usually the easiest — spirits are high in anticipation of cheeseburgers and sofas less than a day away — but this one was the hardest for me. Chilly in my little Mountain Hardwear bag, and the women next to us had an emotional, nature-healing breakup crying session in the late hours. She was clearly going through it, but to do so in such proximity…

## Day 4

Awoke eager, and we made quick time back down to Glen Alpine. There's a little triangle intersection where the PCT meets the Glen Alpine Trail, and I deliberately didn't walk the hypotenuse, despite the fact that it would shave off twenty minutes. Potentially foolish, but I knew I'd be back — Desolation simply has too much to offer. Partially as a move to try and beat Labor Day traffic, we made good time and were home by midday.
